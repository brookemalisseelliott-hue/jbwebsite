Trex vs TimberTech vs Fiberon: which composite decking holds up in Texas?

What each brand actually sells, composite versus PVC, how hot the boards get in an Austin summer, what each tier costs installed, and the board we would put on most backyards here.

By John Bernard·Updated October 5, 2026·8 min read

Almost everyone who asks us about composite decking has already narrowed it to three names: Trex, TimberTech and Fiberon. It is a fair shortlist. All three make good boards, all three are easy to get in Austin, and you will not regret any of them if the deck is built right.

The honest answer to "which is best" is that the brand matters less than people think. The difference between an entry line and a top line inside one brand is bigger than the difference between the brands at the same level. In Austin, the things that decide whether you love the deck in five years are the board's color, whether it is composite or PVC, and how well the frame under it was built.

Here is how we would think it through.

The three brands at a glance

Product names and colors change most years, so treat the line names as a map rather than a shopping list. What stays constant is the structure: every brand has a value line, a middle line most people end up choosing, and a premium line with deeper color and longer fade coverage.

Trex

Trex is the name most people know and it is composite only. Enhance is the value line, Select is the middle, and Transcend and Signature are the top. The newer twist is a heat-reflective cap Trex calls SunComfortable, sold on Transcend Lineage and on some Select and Enhance colors. Trex says those boards run up to 35 degrees cooler than its older boards, which matters more in Austin than almost anywhere.

TimberTech

TimberTech sells both capped composite and what it calls Advanced PVC, a board with no wood in it at all. If you want PVC, TimberTech has the widest range of the three, and its PVC lines are its premium end. The composite lines cover the same value to mid ground as Trex.

Fiberon

Fiberon gets less attention but competes at every tier. Good Life is the value composite, Sanctuary the middle and Concordia the premium composite, with Paramount and Promenade as PVC. At the same tier, Fiberon is often the best value of the three, which is why we always price it alongside the other two.

Composite or PVC: the choice that actually matters

Composite boards are a blend of plastic and wood fiber wrapped in a hard plastic cap. PVC boards are plastic all the way through, with no wood fiber. Both look like wood from a few feet away. They behave differently.

  • PVC is lighter and shrugs off water. With no wood fiber inside, there is nothing to absorb moisture. That makes it our first choice around pools and in spots that stay wet.
  • Composite feels more like wood. It is denser and a bit heavier underfoot, and it usually costs less at the same look.
  • Both move with temperature. Plastic expands in heat and shrinks in cold, and an Austin deck can see a big swing between a January morning and an August afternoon. The gaps at board ends and the fastening pattern have to follow the manufacturer's instructions exactly, or you get gaps, bowing and a voided warranty.

If your deck is near a pool, our pool deck cost guide goes deeper on surfaces that work wet and barefoot.

Heat: the Austin question

Every composite and PVC board gets hotter than wood in direct sun. That is the single biggest complaint we hear about composite here, and it is worth being straight about.

Color matters more than brand. A light gray or tan board is far more comfortable in July than a dark brown or charcoal one from the same line. If the deck gets full afternoon sun and you like to walk on it barefoot, pick from the lightest third of the color chart.

Heat-reflective caps help. Trex's SunComfortable boards are the most widely available version right now. Ask to see the specific color in the sun before you commit, because the difference between colors is still larger than the difference between technologies.

Shade beats all of it. On a west-facing yard with no trees, a patio cover over part of the deck does more for comfort than any board. That is why we often suggest building the cover first and the deck second.

On a 300 square foot deck, the middle of the value range and the middle of the premium range are about $7,500 apart. That is real money, but it is smaller than the jump from pressure-treated pine to any composite. The first decision is wood or composite. The brand comes after.

Brand differences that are real

  • Color range. This is the most common deciding factor in practice. People pick a color they love and the brand follows.
  • PVC choice. If you want PVC, TimberTech and Fiberon both make it and Trex does not.
  • Heat-reflective options. Trex currently has the most colors with a heat-reflective cap.
  • Warranty length. Coverage varies by line rather than by brand. Value lines carry shorter fade and stain coverage, and premium and PVC lines carry the longest. Read the warranty for the exact line, and keep your receipt and install date.
  • Matching railing and fasteners. Each brand has its own hidden fastener system and railing. Mixing brands works, but staying in one system keeps the colors consistent and the install by the book.

What matters more than the brand

Composite decks usually fail for the same reason wood decks do: what is underneath. The boards sit on a wood frame, and the frame is where most problems start.

  • The frame is still pressure-treated wood. It should be built to outlast the boards on top, with joist spacing that meets the board manufacturer's instructions, which is often tighter than for wood decking.
  • Footings for our soil. East of Austin the clay moves with the weather, and in the Hill Country the rock sits close to the surface. Footings have to suit the ground they are in. Our pages for Cedar Park decks, Round Rock decks and deck installation in Buda explain what that means locally.
  • Airflow under the deck. Low ground-level decks need enough ventilation underneath, or the boards and frame stay damp.
  • Installed to the manual. Gaps, fasteners and spacing that ignore the instructions are the most common reason a warranty claim gets turned down.

Our pick

For most Austin backyards, we would choose a mid-tier capped composite in a light color, from whichever brand has the shade you like best. It is the sweet spot of price, looks and comfort.

  • Pool decks and full, all-day sun: PVC in a light color.
  • Covered decks: heat stops mattering much, so pick on color and budget. A value line is often all you need.
  • Budget first, or selling within a few years: a well-built pressure-treated pine deck. Our deck resale value guide explains why the cheaper deck often returns more at sale.

Composite decking questions, answered

Is Trex better than TimberTech?

Not across the board. Both make good capped boards, and the gap between an entry line and a top line inside one brand is bigger than the gap between the brands. TimberTech has a larger range of PVC boards. Trex has a heat-reflective cap on some colors. Pick the line and color first, then the brand.

Is Fiberon as good as Trex?

For most backyards, yes. Fiberon makes capped composite (Good Life, Sanctuary, Concordia) and PVC (Paramount, Promenade), and its mid lines compete directly with Trex Select and TimberTech's mid composite lines. Fiberon is often the better value at the same tier, so it is worth pricing.

Which composite decking stays coolest in Texas sun?

Color matters more than brand. Light grays and tans stay noticeably cooler than dark browns and charcoals. Trex sells some colors with a heat-reflective cap it calls SunComfortable and says those run up to 35 degrees cooler than its older boards. In full afternoon sun, shade over the deck does more than any board.

How much does a composite deck cost in Austin?

Installed composite or PVC decking runs $60 to $100 per square foot in Austin, so a typical 300 square foot deck is $18,000 to $30,000. Entry lines sit at the low end and PVC and top composite lines at the high end. Pressure-treated pine is $30 to $45 per square foot for comparison.

What is the difference between composite and PVC decking?

Composite is a mix of plastic and wood fiber with a protective plastic cap. PVC has no wood in it at all. PVC is lighter and handles water best, which makes it a strong choice around pools. Composite feels a little more like wood underfoot and usually costs less.

Is composite decking worth it over wood in Austin?

If you want low maintenance and plan to stay, usually yes. Composite costs roughly twice as much as pressure-treated pine up front, but it never needs staining. If budget comes first or you may sell soon, a well-built pressure-treated deck is often the smarter money.

Does composite decking fade in the Texas sun?

Capped boards fade far less than older uncapped composite, but every board lightens a little in the first months of hard sun before it settles. Higher lines generally carry longer fade and stain coverage. Check the warranty for the exact line and color you are buying.

Can you build a composite deck on my existing frame?

Sometimes. Composite often needs closer joist spacing than the wood deck it replaces, especially for diagonal boards, and the frame has to be sound. We check the framing and footings first and tell you honestly whether it can carry new boards or should be rebuilt.
